# Patient and companion self-report of deterioration, and digital ED waiting-room tools: evidence for Recheck's base check-in

Scope: adults; research current to 2026-11-07. Read with `docs/research/recheck-waiting-room.md` §§3a, 4 and `question-bank/SOURCES.md`. Some sources could only be reached through search summaries or abstracts. NHS England statistics pages and PubMed pages would not load through the fetch tool. Those items are marked **[abstract only]** or **[via search summary]**, and the primary page should be checked before anyone quotes them.

## Q1. Wellness questions: how valid is "How are you feeling?" and "Compared with before?", what about patient/family worry, and which answer scale fits?

### Takeaway
The two-item Patient Wellness Questionnaire (PWQ) has one small positive study: 103 oncology inpatients, nurse-asked, ratings recorded at only 14% of observations. In that study, "How are you feeling?" predicted the next NEWS, and the combination with "worse/much worse" predicted NEWS 24 h later. A 2026 Welsh feasibility study using the change item found **no** association with NEWS direction. It also found the scale inaccessible to patients with cognitive impairment, speech/language difficulties or critical illness. No study has tested the PWQ in an ED waiting room, by self-completion on a phone, or against hard outcomes. Treat it as a structured voice-of-the-patient signal, not a validated deterioration predictor. Its only defensible actionable output is "worse/much worse".

### Cited Findings
- **PWQ wording and scale (original):** Item 1 "How are you feeling?" was scored very poor / poor / fair / good / very good. Item 2 "How are you feeling compared with the last time you were asked?" was scored much worse / worse / no change / better / much better. There was **no "not sure" option**; staff could record that the patient refused or was too unwell to answer. — [Albutt et al., J Patient Saf 2021, PMC8612917](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)
- **Who asked:** Nurses and healthcare assistants asked the questions during routine observations, prompted by a laminated card, and entered answers in an e-obs app. Patients did not self-complete. — [Albutt 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)
- **Design and sample:** Prospective observational study on 4 oncology wards in one Northern England hospital over 4 weeks (Sept 2017). 69 staff took part, and ratings were recorded for 103 of 125 patients (mean age 66, range 39–90). — [Albutt 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)
- **Completion:** A wellness rating was recorded at **14% of observations** (3–55% per patient). Overall missing data were 18% (listwise deletion), and staff rarely recorded reasons for missing ratings. — [Albutt 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)
- **Results (controlling for baseline NEWS):** Item 1 predicted NEWS at the next observation (B = 0.180, SE 0.083, standardized 0.36, p = 0.033). Item 2 alone and the combined score did not. At ~24 h, the combined score (Item 1 × Item 2) predicted NEWS (B = 0.229, p = 0.005), but neither item alone did. Item 1 was associated with later NEWS only when patients also said "worse/much worse" (B = 0.197, p = 0.049). The paper labels unstandardized coefficients as β, which is inconsistent with Table 2. — [Albutt 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)
- **Authors' limits:** Small, single site, preliminary, possible selection bias in which observations got ratings, no diagnosis/treatment data, opt-out recruitment. They state that larger studies are needed before clinical use. — [Albutt 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)
- **Development study:** Uptake was limited when patients were asked to record their own PWQ ratings. When a researcher asked and recorded the answers, the process was acceptable to most patients. — [Albutt et al., J Res Nurs 2020, PMC7932225](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7932225/)
- **2026 replication/feasibility (Wales):** Subbe et al. ran a mixed-methods study on 4 medical wards in one hospital. A verbal "Patient Wellness Scale" ("How are you feeling compared to the last time we asked you?", 5 options) got 172 responses from 448 patients (38%), and responses showed **no association with direction of NEWS change** (χ², p > 0.05). Reasons for non-response were asleep 82, away from bedside 41, infection control 44, delirium 16, dementia 11. In the electronic arm the field was documented for 22/40 patients, and one HCA filled it for 4 patients without asking them. The scale "was not accessible to patients with speech and language difficulties, cognitive impairments, dementia and those who were critically unwell". The authors warn it could be implemented "mechanistically". Limitations: single site, no outcome data, first author led the QI project, and dates are inconsistent in the methods. — [Subbe et al., Front Health Serv 2026, PMC13553874](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/health-services/articles/10.3389/frhs.2026.1854717/full)
- **Larger outcome study planned, no results found:** An HRA/NIHR-registered study aims to test whether patient-reported wellness predicts cardiac arrest, unplanned ICU transfer, mortality and time to discharge. I found no published results. — [HRA research summary](https://www.hra.nhs.uk/planning-and-improving-research/application-summaries/research-summaries/clinical-effectiveness-of-the-patient-wellness-questionnaire/); [NIHR PSRC Yorkshire & Humber](https://psrc-yh.nihr.ac.uk/research-themes-partners/patient-involvement-in-patient-safety/our-projects/improving-early-detection-of-clinical-deterioration-in-hospital/)
- **Parent-reported wellness in a paediatric ED (indirect; children):** n = 407. Parent-reported child wellness (PWQ-P) was the strongest independent predictor of the POPS early-warning score (B = 0.223, β = 0.202, p < 0.001), but total R² was only 0.053. About 10% of children with no physiological derangement were rated "very poorly" by parents. — [J Patient Saf 2022, PMC9329037](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9329037/)
- **Unverified "24 hours earlier" claim:** An advocacy page claims PWQ answers detect deterioration "up to 24 hours before" vital signs. That overstates the primary paper, which reported an association with NEWS 24 h later in one small cohort. — [World Patients Alliance page](https://www.worldpatientsalliance.org/recognizing-and-responding-to-a-deteriorating-patient/patient-wellness-questionnaire/) vs [Albutt 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)
- **Nurse "worry" as a criterion:** A systematic review of 18 studies found nurses' worry/concern can precede deterioration, with or without changed vital signs. Whether worry as a calling criterion improves outcomes is unclear. — [Douw et al., Crit Care 2015, PMC4461986](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4461986/) **[via search summary + abstract]**
- **Family/caregiver concern:** NSW CEC guidance for adults asks families "are they getting worse?". It notes that a "yes" is uncommon but an important early signal, and that in paediatrics caregiver concern was more often associated with critical illness than any single abnormal vital sign. — [CEC NSW, "Worried they are getting worse" (adults)](https://cec.health.nsw.gov.au/safety-essentials/deterioration/worried-they-are-getting-worse/adults) **[via search summary]**
- **Expert implementation guidance:** It argues that Martha's Rule needs both escalation by patients/families and routine input of the patient's own view of wellness and trajectory into the record, and it proposes metrics. It is expert consensus, not outcome data. — [Future Healthc J 2025, PMC11889965](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11889965/)

### Inferences
- The predictive signal sits in **"worse/much worse"** and in the absolute "how are you feeling" item, not in "better/no change". This supports Recheck's rule that a reassuring answer cannot clear a concern, and suggests routing only on "worse/much worse" (or "very poor") rather than on small shifts.
- The 5-point scale matches the source. Adding "Not sure" is a reasonable adaptation for self-completion. The PWQ has no equivalent, so it must be stored and handled as *unknown*, not as "no change". Keep "unable to answer" (observer-reported) distinct from both.
- Recheck adapts Item 2 to "compared with when staff last checked on you". That anchors the comparison to a clinical contact rather than to the last prompt, so it is not the validated wording. Neither wording has ED evidence. The Albutt finding that Item 2 alone did not predict NEWS means the change item should not be the sole trigger.
- Both hospital studies show the questions are answered far less often than intended (14–38%), even when nurses ask face-to-face. Missingness should therefore be a reported metric, not a surprise.

### Gaps
- No study of PWQ-type questions in adult ED waiting rooms, by self-completed phone/web, or against ED outcomes (deterioration in waiting room, admission, ICU, death).
- I did not retrieve adult ED studies of single-item self-rated health or patient "gut feeling" predicting short-term outcomes. Searches returned only off-topic hits, so this remains unconfirmed rather than absent.
- No study compares 5-point wellness scales with or without a "not sure" option.

## Q2. Pain: is 0–10 NRS self-report valid in the ED, is repeating it useful while waiting, and what wording?

### Takeaway
The verbally administered 0–10 NRS is validated against VAS in ED adults (r = 0.94), with a minimum clinically significant difference of about 1.3–1.4 points. Its strength is tracking change within a person, not ranking urgency between people. In a 2026 ED self-triage study, higher self-reported pain was strongly associated with **overtriage**, so pain score alone should not raise acuity. I found no ED evidence that repeated NRS in the waiting room detects deterioration.

### Cited Findings
- **ED validation:** A prospective convenience sample of 108 ED adults with acute pain (103 at 30 min, 86 at 60 min) used the anchors "**0 = no pain, 10 = worst possible pain**". NRS vs VAS gave r = 0.94 (95% CI 0.93–0.95), slope 1.01. MCSD was **1.3 (1.0–1.5)** on the NRS, defined from "a little more/a little less" ratings. The authors concluded the verbal NRS can substitute for the VAS. — [Bijur et al., Acad Emerg Med 2003, PMID 12670856](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12670856/) (abstract retrieved via Europe PMC)
- **Other MCSD estimate:** 1.41 points (SD ± 1.05) on a patient-assigned 11-point scale. — [Ann Emerg Med (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0196064404010066) **[via search summary]**
- **Validation quality caveat:** One large study notes that few prior studies, using questionable methods, support the ED verbal NRS. — [Pain 2008, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304395908000705) **[via search summary]**
- **Pain inflates self-triage urgency:** 2,274 stable adult walk-ins used a tablet app based on Manchester Triage System logic, with blinded nurse MTS as comparator. The app overtriaged 51.1% and undertriaged 6.3% (sens 93.9%, spec 28.7%). **Higher self-reported pain intensity was strongly associated with overtriage**, and overtriage exceeded 70% for headache and low back pain. 29.9% of registrations were excluded because their complaints weren't covered. — [J Clin Med 2026, PMC13566270](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13566270/) **[abstract only]**
- **Language-barrier alternative:** An Italian single-centre pilot is testing an emoji-based acute pain scale for ED patients with language barriers. — [J Clin Med 2026, PMC13566320](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13566320/) **[abstract only; results not read]**
- **Proxy pain agreement:** Caregiver-patient agreement was better for pain (ICC 0.58) than for anxiety/depression (0.25–0.28). That supports the current choice to ask pain of the patient only, though proxies are not useless. — [J Patient Rep Outcomes 2022, PMC9107556](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9107556/)

### Inferences
- Keep NRS as a patient-only trend item. Flag a **rise of ≥2 points** (above the ~1.3 MCSD) or a jump to 9–10 for staff review in the waiting-room worklist. Do not let pain alone push a patient to urgent physician routing, except within complaint-specific rules such as chest/back pain that moves or sharply worsens.
- Bijur's anchor wording, "worst possible pain", is the anchor with ED validation behind it. "Worst pain you can imagine" is a common variant, but I found no source establishing either as *the* standard.
- A functional/comparative companion item (e.g., Bijur's own "much less / a little less / about the same / a little more / much more") is how the MCSD was anchored. It could replace or accompany a second NRS for simpler reading.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on repeated waiting-room NRS as a deterioration signal, or on verbal rating scales vs NRS specifically in ED waiting rooms.
- A 2026 systematic review on pain's impact on ED triage accuracy ([Arch Acad Emerg Med 2026, PMC13637428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13637428/)) was found, but its abstract was not retrievable. Read it before finalising pain rules.

## Q3. Proxy/companion reporting: how well do companions report symptoms, confusion and drowsiness?

### Takeaway
At group level, companions approximate patient reports for pain and physical symptoms, but agreement is weaker for psychological states. Caregivers **under-report** when the patient's symptoms are severe. For new neurological deficits and confusion, observers add information the patient cannot give. In one cohort, 6.6% of TIA patients were unaware of symptoms that bystanders witnessed, and they were higher risk. Single informant questions ("more confused lately?") have modest sensitivity (44–77%). The structured FAM-CAM performs better. A companion's "worse" is informative, while a companion's "fine" is not reassuring.

### Cited Findings
- **TIA witnessed by bystanders:** In the PROMISE-TIA multicentre registry (1,414 clinical TIAs; 896 without imaging lesions analysed; median age 70), **59 (6.6%) were unaware of their TIA symptoms but had them witnessed by bystanders**. These patients were older, more often female, with more prior stroke, premorbid disability and AF. They arrived earlier and had a higher ABCD² (median 5 vs 4). Unawareness predicted 1-year ischaemic stroke (HR 2.44, 95% CI 1.10–4.83), but not after adjusting for arterial stenosis/occlusion. — [Eur J Neurol 2021, PMID 32961590](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32961590/) (abstract via Europe PMC)
- **Older adult–caregiver agreement (telephone PROs):** 188 dyads plus 200 patients without caregivers. ICC was 0.58 for pain (PEG), 0.48 for SymTrak-23 symptoms, 0.28 for anxiety and 0.25 for depression. Higher patient-reported severity was associated with caregiver **under-reporting**, and higher caregiver task difficulty with over-reporting. Interpret proxy reports cautiously for individuals with severe or psychological symptoms. — [J Patient Rep Outcomes 2022, PMC9107556](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9107556/)
- **ICU proxies:** Family members reported higher symptom intensity than patients for 80% of symptoms. Proxies can help but both over- and under-estimate. — [PubMed 22890258](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22890258/) **[via search summary]**
- **SQiD ("Do you feel that [name] has been more confused lately?"):** In 2 cancer centres, 73 patients had SQiD plus psychiatrist interview. Sensitivity was **44%**, specificity **87%**, NPV 74%, κ = 0.32. — [BMC Cancer 2021, PMC7814717](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7814717/). An earlier n = 21 study reported 80% sensitivity / 71% specificity with very wide CIs. — [academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/1492916/Single_question_in_delirium_SQiD_testing_its_efficacy_against_psychiatrist_interview_the_Confusion_Assessment_Method_and_the_Memorial_Delirium_Assessment_Scale) **[via search summary]**
- **Informant single question for delirium:** "How has your relative/friend's memory changed with his/her current illness?" was asked on a 5-point Likert scale of older inpatients (median age 80.8). Informants were obtainable for only **70/161 (43.5%)**. Against CAM, sensitivity was 76.9% and specificity 56.1%, less accurate than for dementia. — [BMC Geriatr 2015, PMC4345037](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4345037/)
- **Caregiver tools, systematic review:** Caregiver-*administered* tools outperformed caregiver-*informed* single questions. FAM-CAM had sensitivity 75% (95% CI 35–95) and specificity 91% (74–97). Copies of the review attach figures inconsistently, so check the original before quoting. — [Rosgen et al., J Am Geriatr Soc 2018](https://agsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jgs.15362) **[via search summary]**
- **FAM-CAM across a language barrier (ICU, 2026):** 142 patients and 100 caregivers. Among Spanish speakers, usual-care CAM-ICU agreed poorly with the reference (κ 0.11, missing 72% of delirium). Caregiver-administered FAM-CAM gave κ 0.68 and cut missed delirium by 47%. — [Crit Care Med 2026, PMC13185727](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13185727/) **[abstract only]**. A companion substudy found FAM-CAM engagement raised caregiver satisfaction (91.9 vs 84.4). — [Intensive Care Med 2026, PMC12971838](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12971838/)
- **Delirium is common in ED older adults:** A meta-analysis (11 studies, 2020–2026) found 20.6% prevalence, with hypoactive the most common subtype and the worst prognosis. — [Brain Sci 2026, PMC13511352](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13511352/) **[abstract only]**
- **Stroke proxies:** An updated systematic review found proxies mostly overestimate ADL/QoL impairment after stroke. — [J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis 2024](https://www.strokejournal.org/article/S1052-3057(24)00145-9/fulltext) **[via search summary]**

### Inferences
- Companion questions should ask about **observed change from the patient's usual self** (SQiD/FAM-CAM style: "more confused / harder to wake / speech different than usual?"). Recheck's neuro/head-injury/confused groups already do this, and that design is supported.
- Hypoactive delirium is the common, high-risk ED subtype. Drowsiness or "harder to keep awake" belongs among the companion warning signs.
- Because informants were available for fewer than half of older inpatients in one study, the staff schedule must remain the safety net, as the brief already says.

### Gaps
- No ED-waiting-room study of companion vs patient agreement for acute symptom change, and none of companion-reported FAST signs by phone.
- FAM-CAM is longer (11 items) and was not evaluated for repeated short check-ins. Its licensing was not checked.

## Q4. Self-administered cognitive/attention tasks (DelApp-style): valid for acutely unwell adults? Should `circle_count` stay?

### Takeaway
DelApp, a star-counting task gated by an arousal assessment, discriminated delirium well (AUC 0.89) in a case-control study of older inpatients. In every published validation, **a trained assessor administered it**. Patients with severe visual impairment and those who could not understand spoken English were excluded. No study tests self-administration on a personal phone in an ED waiting room. Unsupervised smartphone cognitive tests have been studied in healthy, motivated registry volunteers, not acutely ill patients. The patients most likely to fail a self-test (delirious, drowsy, low vision, low literacy, non-English) are also the least able to complete an app. So a missing or wrong count is uninterpretable, and a correct count is not reassuring.

### Cited Findings
- **DelApp case-control DTA:** 187 analysed (61 delirium, 61 dementia without delirium, 65 no impairment), mean age 83.8, on Glasgow/Edinburgh medical and rehabilitation wards in 2015–16. **Psychology graduates administered it, blinded.** Task: count white stars on a black screen while ignoring distractor triangles, over 7 trials (the first unscored), stopping after 2 consecutive errors. An arousal score (0–4) gates attention (0–6), total 0–10, ≤5 min. — [Tieges et al., PLOS One 2020, PMC6980392](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227471)
- **Accuracy:** Medians were delirium 4.0, dementia 9.0, no impairment 10.0. AUC 0.89 (0.84–0.94). At cut ≤8, sensitivity was 91.7% and specificity 74.2%. Delirium vs dementia: AUC 0.80, specificity **68.3%** at ≤6. Specificity vs dementia fell to **59.4%** in severe cognitive impairment. — [PLOS One 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227471)
- **DelApp limitations (authors):** The case-control design may overstate accuracy, about half the delirium group had dementia, and inter-rater reliability was not assessed. Exclusions included severe visual impairment, and spoken English was required. Self-administration and dexterity were not discussed. — [PLOS One 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227471)
- **Cohort protocol:** The prospective multicentre validation also used a blinded assessor to administer DelApp. — [Protocol, PubMed 30223771](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30223771/); [NCT02590796](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02590796) **[via search summary]**
- **Unsupervised smartphone cognitive testing:** Tested in 1,815 adults *without* known cognitive impairment from the UCSF Brain Health Registry (ages 18–92). These were community volunteers, not acute patients. — [Alzheimers Dement 2026, PMC13254688](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13254688/) **[abstract only, results not read]**
- **Self-report fails in the same patients:** The Wales PWS study found even a single spoken wellness question inaccessible to patients with cognitive impairment, dementia or speech/language problems, or who were critically unwell. — [Front Health Serv 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/health-services/articles/10.3389/frhs.2026.1854717/full)
- **Recommended ED screen is clinician-administered:** In the ED delirium meta-analysis, the 4AT was the most used screening tool. — [Brain Sci 2026, PMC13511352](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13511352/)

### Inferences
- `circle_count` as a patient self-test has **no direct validity evidence**. Its likely failure modes are false positives (low vision, small screens, glare, unfamiliarity, distraction in a busy waiting room, low literacy/numeracy, anxiety) and uninformative non-completion (the delirious or drowsy patient doesn't open the prompt). Neither produces a clean signal. Each false positive adds a non-urgent staff task.
- A task framed as a "test" may worry patients and could be read as a judgement on their competence. No study measured this. It is an inference.
- **Recommendation:** remove it from the base patient check-in. Put a companion observation item ("more confused, muddled or harder to keep awake than usual?") in the base or confused/neuro groups, and keep any attention test in the **staff** workflow (4AT or a DelApp-style task run by staff). If the team wants to keep `circle_count` for learning, run it only as an opt-in, research-flagged item outside routing, with explicit capture of "couldn't see / didn't want to / didn't finish", and measure its false-positive load before it creates tasks.

### Gaps
- No studies on self-administered attention tasks in ED or acutely ill adults, on anxiety from in-app cognitive tests, or on DelApp licensing (unknown, per SOURCES.md).

## Q5. Digital ED waiting-room tools: eTriage, SMS/virtual waiting rooms, wait information, LWBS, uptake and digital exclusion

### Takeaway
Patient self-triage tools are sensitive but heavily overtriage relative to nurses, with 51–65% overtriage across studies. That is the main workload risk for any patient-input tool. SMS "wait elsewhere and be recalled" systems for **low-acuity** patients are well liked and, in one Canadian press-reported pilot, coincided with lower LWBS. None of them monitored symptoms or deterioration. I found **no published study of a phone-based serial symptom reassessment tool in an ED waiting room**, so Recheck's core function is untested in the literature. Digital tools excluded non-covered complaints, non-local languages and people without phones.

### Cited Findings
- **UK eTriage:** A retrospective study over 8 months in 2 EDs with ambulatory adults ≥18: of 43,788 attendees, 26,757 used eTriage (~61%), giving 25,333 paired triages. Weighted κ was 0.14 vs nurse MTS, undertriage 10.1% and **overtriage 59.2%**. Sensitivity for high-acuity outcomes was 88.5% (eTriage) vs 53.8% (nurse MTS), and specificity for low risk 88.5% vs 80.6%. — [Eur J Emerg Med 2022, PMID 34545027](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34545027/) (abstract via Europe PMC)
- **Systematic review of check-in/triage kiosks:** 5 studies (47,778 patients, 310,249 visits). Sensitivity for high acuity reached 88.5%, undertriage 8.0–10.1%, agreement with nurse triage 27.0–30.7%, **overtriage 59.2–65.0%**, with mixed effects on waits. One study reported 97% uptake. The authors judged the evidence sparse. — [JMIR 2025, PMC12138304](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12138304/)
- **Mobile MTS self-triage app (2026):** Overtriage 51.1%, undertriage 6.3%, 29.9% of registrations not covered by pathways, and pain-driven overtriage (see Q2). — [J Clin Med 2026, PMC13566270](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13566270/)
- **Geneva SMS recall (wait elsewhere):** Adult outpatient emergency unit, triage levels 3–4, French-speaking, 100 surveyed patients (convenience sample) and 10/25 nurses. The nurse sends the recall text, with reminders every 20 min, up to 3. Results: 97% satisfied with the service, 56% satisfied with wait time, 92% had enough time to return, 72% not particularly stressed. LWBS and safety were **not measured**, there was no control group, the uptake rate was not recorded, and several authors hold IP. Nurses rated it easy (6.0/7) but reported extra workload. Enrolment grew from 46/month to 546/month over 2017–2019. — [JMIR Form Res 2022, PMC9490523](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9490523/)
- **Sault Area Hospital (Ontario) virtual home waiting room (from Aug 2025):** Non-urgent patients wait at home with hourly text updates on queue position. Press reports say LWBS was **13% among non-users vs 3% among users**, with ~500 users and 87% "very satisfied". This was not peer-reviewed, users were self-selected, and it covered low acuity only. — [SAH page](https://sah.on.ca/programs-services/emergency-services/virtual-home-waiting-room/); [Maclean's](https://macleans.ca/society/health/at-this-er-patients-wait-at-home/); [Canadian Healthcare Technology, Mar 2026](https://www.canhealth.com/2026/03/18/virtual-ed-helps-reduce-waits-for-some-patients/) **[via search summary]**
- **Waiting room to virtual consult (Victoria, Australia):** Retrospective. Of 339 non-urgent waiting-room patients seen by the virtual ED, 76% were diverted. 9% re-presented within 7 days, 3 adverse events caused temporary harm, and there were no deaths. — [Oxf Open Digit Health 2026, PMC13386168](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13386168/)
- **Wait-time information can backfire:** A simulation calibrated on 3 Hong Kong EDs found accurate, frequently updated predictions reduced waits and LWBS. **Inaccurate predictions increased mean wait and LWBS** at high adoption. — [JAMIA 2026, PMC13197183](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13197183/)
- **LWBS drivers:** A scoping review of 94 publications (2020–2025) found LWBS driven by waiting time and crowding, mainly affecting low-acuity, socioeconomically vulnerable adults. Physician-in-triage reduces it. — [Open Access Emerg Med 2026, PMC13615747](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13615747/). In 373,388 visits, 9.3% of LWBS patients were high acuity. — [West J Emerg Med 2025, PMC12698166](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12698166/)
- **Digital exclusion:** Smartphone ownership in patient populations ranges **34–91%** internationally (cited in a 2026 cardiology inpatient study), versus ~90% of Australian adults. — [JMIR Cardio 2026, PMC13229392](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13229392/). A scoping review on age and the digital divide (45 publications) addresses barriers for older adults. — [Front Digit Health 2026, PMC13519148](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13519148/) **[abstract only]**. Victoria's virtual ED added non-clinical "clinical assistants" to support digitally vulnerable patients. — [Front Health Serv 2026, PMC13365251](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13365251/)
- **Language exclusion in the studies themselves:** The Geneva tool was French-speaking only ([PMC9490523](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9490523/)), DelApp required spoken English ([PLOS One 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227471)), and the PFAES review found equity "inconsistently addressed despite language and communication barriers being recurrent" ([IJQHC 2026, PMC13458593](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13458593/)).
- **Contactless monitoring is a separate track:** A camera-based vital-sign monitoring pilot after triage in Australian ED walk-ins (2025) is staff-side technology, not self-report. — [Emerg Med Australas 2026, PMC13439330](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13439330/) **[abstract only]**

### Inferences
- Expect patient-input alerts to over-call. Recheck's tiering helps: the urgent route goes only for specific warning signs, and everything else becomes a non-urgent worklist item. Alert-volume measurement should be a pilot outcome, as the brief says.
- Recheck shows queue/wait information, so it should show ranges and update them, or show nothing, rather than an inaccurate point estimate.
- The LWBS benefits came from *letting low-acuity people leave the building*. Recheck keeps people in the waiting room, so those satisfaction/LWBS results should not be claimed for it.

### Gaps
- No published study of serial phone/SMS **symptom** reassessment of patients waiting for an ED physician, and no completion-rate data for such prompts in the ED.
- No Canadian ED-specific data on smartphone access by age or language were found.

## Q6. Frequency and burden: how often will patients answer, and how many questions per check-in?

### Takeaway
Repeated-prompt compliance can be high in motivated, stable populations: a 79% pooled rate in oncology EMA, and 99% among healthy Singaporean older volunteers. In-hospital wellness questions, however, were captured at only 14–38% of opportunities. No data exist for ED waiting-room prompts at 15–30 minute intervals. Design for substantial missingness, keep each check-in to a few taps, and do not treat a missed prompt as reassurance.

### Cited Findings
- **Oncology EMA meta-analysis:** 23 studies, 13,565 participants. Pooled compliance was **78.6%** (95% CI 73.5–82.9), with a prediction interval **48.6–93.4%**. Meta-regression found **no association** with prompts per day, items per prompt, study length or response window. GRADE certainty was very low. — [JMIR 2026, PMC13552840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13552840/)
- **Older adults, smartphone EMA:** 130 community-dwelling adults ≥65 in Singapore (65% post-secondary education) received 5 prompts/day for 14 days, each open for 5 h. Compliance was **99%** (9,007/9,100) with 100% retention. They were healthy, educated volunteers, so the result shows feasibility, not generalisability. — [JMIR Form Res 2026, PMC13392534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13392534/)
- **In-hospital wellness prompts:** Ratings were recorded at 14% of observations, nurse-asked ([Albutt 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)), and 38% of patients in the Wales study ([Subbe 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/health-services/articles/10.3389/frhs.2026.1854717/full)). Self-completion uptake was "limited". — [Albutt 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7932225/)
- **Martha's Rule frequency standard:** At least daily. — [Subbe 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/health-services/articles/10.3389/frhs.2026.1854717/full); [NHS England core standards](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/marthas-rule-core-standards/)
- **Comparable ED messaging cadences:** Geneva sent recall reminders every 20 min, up to 3 ([PMC9490523](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9490523/)), and Sault Area Hospital sent queue updates every 60 min ([SAH](https://sah.on.ca/programs-services/emergency-services/virtual-home-waiting-room/)). These were information pushes, not questionnaires.

### Inferences
- A 15-minute patient prompt for CTAS 2 is untested and probably burdensome. CTAS 2 patients should be under staff observation, with the patient app as an adjunct and staff reassessment as primary. 30–60 min prompts are closer to the cadences patients tolerated in ED messaging.
- Minimum per check-in is the two wellness items plus a "new warning signs? none / yes" gate, ideally under ~30 seconds. Pain, complaint follow-ups and other items should appear only if relevant or on alternate check-ins. The EMA meta-regression suggests item count isn't a strong driver of compliance in motivated samples, but acutely unwell ED patients are not that population. This is an inference.
- Report per-visit response rates, time-to-response and "no response" separately from "delivery failure", as the brief requires.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence on optimal prompt frequency, the response-rate curve over time, or the number of items for acutely unwell people waiting in an ED.

## Q7. Martha's Rule and patient/family-initiated escalation: what proportion of calls are clinical, what is the false-alarm load, and what do staff experience?

### Takeaway
About 30% of Martha's Rule calls concern acute deterioration. Of those, a little over half lead to a change in treatment or transfer. Most calls come from families, and many patients' early-warning scores would not have triggered escalation. Older US "Condition Help" data show most patient calls are non-safety issues, commonly pain. Awareness is very low even in mature systems, and patients fear speaking up. Early ED staff reports suggest the workload is manageable, but the evidence is small and survey-based.

### Cited Findings
- **NHS England programme totals:** Sept 2024–July 2026, 19,177 calls, of which 5,660 (30%) concerned acute deterioration. The ED/maternity/neonatal extension has collected data from June 2026, counts <10 are suppressed, and the figures are management information. — [NHS England, Martha's Rule July 2026](https://www.england.nhs.uk/statistics/statistical-work-areas/marthas-rule/statistical-work-areas-marthas-rule-marthas-rule-2026-27/statistical-work-areas-marthas-rule-statistical-work-areas-marthas-rule-marthas-rule-2026-27-marthas-rule-programme-july2026/) **[via search summary; page would not load]**
- **Outcomes of deterioration calls (Feb 2026 report):** Of 4,047 acute-deterioration calls, 43% were advice only, 13% transfer of care and 44% another change in treatment, so ~57% changed care. — [NHS England Feb 2026](https://www.england.nhs.uk/statistics/statistical-work-areas/marthas-rule/marthas-rule-2025-26/marthas-rule-programme-february2026-2/) **[via search summary]**
- **Caller mix and missed-by-NEWS calls:** Sept 2024–May 2026: 72% of escalation calls came from family/carers, ~31% concerned acute deterioration, and in 222 calls that led to a transfer of care the patient's early-warning score would not have triggered escalation. — [NHS England news, Mar 2026](https://www.england.nhs.uk/2026/03/over-10000-calls-to-marthas-rule-in-first-16-months-to-trigger-urgent-review-of-care/) (as summarised in the Recheck brief §3a; page not re-fetched)
- **A&E pilot:** 7 trusts (Sept 2025–Mar 2026) logged 69 calls without extra clinical staff, and rollout to all A&Es, including waiting rooms, is planned by March 2028. — [Nursing Times, Sept 2026](https://www.nursingtimes.net/emergency-and-critical-care/marthas-rule-set-to-be-expanded-to-all-ae-departments-22-09-2026/) (per brief §3a)
- **BMJ news:** "One in five calls under Martha's rule leads to change or escalation" (2025). — [BMJ 2025, PMID 40602811](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40602811/) **[title only]**
- **Condition Help (US, single centre):** Jan 2012–Jun 2015, 367 calls from 240 patients/families. **76.8% were from patients** and 21.8% from family, **83.4% concerned non-safety issues** vs 11.4% safety, and 41.4% led to a change in care. Inadequate pain control was the most common reason. — [Eden et al., J Hosp Med 2017](https://shmpublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.12788/jhm.2697) **[via search summary]**
- **Systematic review of patient/family-activated escalation (2026):** 35 studies. Two models emerged: direct-to-response-team and proxy activation through ward staff. Low awareness (16/35) and limited understanding (15/35) were common. Patients and families feared speaking up and harming relationships, and many preferred staff-led escalation. Direct-to-team escalation is a safety net but "often feels too risky or confrontational". Equity was inconsistently addressed. — [Int J Qual Health Care 2026, PMC13458593](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13458593/)
- **Awareness in a mature system:** At a North Wales district general hospital, more than 2 years after launch and with wide publicity, **93% of 168 patients/relatives were unaware** of the service. Barriers included terminology and fear of "whistleblowing". — [Front Health Serv 2026, PMC13388555](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13388555/)
- **Paediatric ED staff experience:** Pre-implementation, all 17 senior staff expected more workload and 88% expected worse flow. At 6 months, 87% (of 15 respondents, 44% response) said neither had happened. The study was small and survey-based. — [Ir Med J 2026, PMID 41728835](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41728835/)
- **CALD considerations:** A 2026 JBI scoping review of hospital initiatives for patient/carer escalation specifically examines culturally and linguistically diverse populations. — [JBI Evid Synth 2026, PMC13344392](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13344392/) **[abstract only, results not read]**

### Inferences
- About 70% of escalations are not acute deterioration, and pain is the most common reason in Condition Help. This supports Recheck's separate "Question about my wait or care" route and suggests pain/comfort requests go to the triage nurse.
- "False alarm" is the wrong frame: in the NHS data, ~43% of deterioration calls ended in advice only, but advice is still a response. Measure the load as calls per 100 waiting patients and clinician minutes per call.
- Low awareness and fear of speaking up mean an always-visible in-app button is likely to be used more than a phone number on a poster. That is an inference with no direct comparison. Wording that sounds legitimate and non-confrontational ("I'm worried I'm getting worse") matters.

### Gaps
- No published ED-specific Martha's Rule outcome data yet (collection began June 2026), and no data on in-app vs phone activation.

**Bottom line for the report writer:** No study has evaluated serial phone-based self-report for adults waiting for an ED physician, so Recheck's base check-in rests on indirect, mostly small studies. The defensible design is a short check-in (two wellness items, a warning-sign gate, and pain for patients) whose "worse" answers raise review and whose reassuring or missing answers never clear it. Pair it with always-visible escalation buttons and a staff schedule that carries the safety net. The self-administered circle-count is the weakest-supported element.

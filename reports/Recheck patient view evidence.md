# Make the waiting-room phone screen say "go to staff"

Recheck's patient and companion screen should be **three always-visible buttons on top of a short, honest check-in**. The buttons are "I need help now", "I'm worried I'm getting worse" and a non-clinical question route. The check-in is two wellness questions, a list of observable warning signs with an explicit "None of these", and pain for the patient only. Every screen after a report should repeat the Alberta instruction that already exists in policy: if you feel worse, go to the triage desk now. The evidence supports the **escalation buttons** best. Martha's Rule programme data show families make most calls, and many deterioration calls came when the early-warning score would not have triggered. The **wellness questions** rest on one small inpatient study and a 2026 replication that found no signal. The **self-administered circle count** has no validation for self-use, and no published study has tested serial phone self-report by adults waiting for an ED physician. Recheck's design should therefore treat "worse" answers as reasons for review and never treat reassuring or missing answers as clearance, which the current rules already do. The current question bank is mostly sound. It needs six plain-language rewordings, a fix to the stroke onset question, and a reviewed set of new universal warning signs: severe breathlessness, skin colour change, face or throat swelling, seizure, and coughing or vomiting blood. The patient copy has four safety-relevant defects to fix tonight. The offline urgent state does not say staff don't know. The desk screen tells people they need do nothing else. The place-in-line promise is broader than AHS policy. The wait estimate misstates what AHS measures. The research brief also cites a Martha's Rule figure ("222 calls") that could not be found in any source.

## Martha's Rule transfers as a button, not as a validated questionnaire

Martha's Rule has three parts: a structured wellness question asked at least daily, 24/7 access for patients, families and staff to a rapid review, and review by a team outside the treating team ([NHS England, programme statistics](https://www.england.nhs.uk/statistics/statistical-work-areas/marthas-rule/marthas-rule-2025-26/marthas-rule-programme-october-2025/) [snippet]). NHS England published six core standards on 2 March 2026. The canonical page could not be retrieved, so their exact text and any response-time standard remain **unverified**. No numeric "review within X minutes" target was found anywhere. Recheck's patient copy should therefore promise no timed response, and the current copy promises none. The extension to emergency care is real. A seven-trust A&E pilot (September 2025 to March 2026) logged **69 calls without extra clinical staff**, and rollout to every A&E, including waiting rooms, is due by March 2028 ([Nursing Times](https://www.nursingtimes.net/emergency-and-critical-care/marthas-rule-set-to-be-expanded-to-all-ae-departments-22-09-2026/); [EMJ](https://www.emjreviews.com/general-healthcare/news/marthas-rule-to-expand-across-englands-aes/)). The UK route is a phone line used after first speaking to the care team, and the pilot trusts are unnamed. No ED-specific wellness question has been published. That much friction means UK pilot volumes cannot predict how often a one-tap in-app button will be pressed.

The family route has the strongest support. Across September 2024 to June 2026, **72% of Martha's Rule calls came through the family/carer route** and about **30% concerned acute deterioration** ([NHS England Martha's Rule](https://www.england.nhs.uk/patient-safety/marthas-rule/) [snippet]). Among deterioration calls from June 2025 to mid-2026, **82% came when the early-warning score would not otherwise have triggered escalation**, and 13–14% of those led to transfer of care and 55–56% to another treatment change (same source [snippet]). The March 2026 release, read through a trade-press mirror, gives 10,119 calls from September 2024 to December 2025, 34% of them acute deterioration ([Clinical Services Journal](https://www.clinicalservicesjournal.com/story/50907/over-10-000-calls-to-marthas-rule-in-first-16-months-to-trigger-urgent-review-of)). This is operational data with no comparator, but it is the best evidence that a person's worry adds signal beyond physiology. Leeds's Call 4 Concern page shows how trusts describe family triggers in observable terms. It lists general worry, the patient seeming "not themselves", breathing differently, and hands that feel cold or very hot ([Leeds Teaching Hospitals](https://www.leedsth.nhs.uk/patients/call-4-concern-marthas-rule/)). Recheck's companion copy uses none of these cues yet.

The scheduled wellness question is much weaker. The Patient Wellness Questionnaire asks "How are you feeling?" (very good to very poor) and "How are you feeling compared with the last time you were asked?" (much better to much worse). In **103 oncology inpatients**, with nurses asking and ratings recorded at only **14% of observations**, the first item predicted the next NEWS, and the product of the two items predicted NEWS about 24 hours later. The authors call the result preliminary ([Albutt et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612917/)). A 2026 Welsh feasibility study asked the change item verbally and got 172 responses from 448 patients. It found **no association with the direction of NEWS change**, and the scale was inaccessible to patients with cognitive impairment, speech or language difficulties, or critical illness ([Subbe et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/health-services/articles/10.3389/frhs.2026.1854717/full)). When patients were asked to record ratings themselves, uptake was limited ([Albutt et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7932225/)). Three consequences follow for Recheck. Only "worse/much worse" and "very poor" carry signal worth routing. "Not sure" is a legitimate self-completion adaptation, but it must be stored as unknown, never as "no change", and the bank already does this. Missing answers will be the norm, so the staff timer must carry the safety net.

Non-clinical traffic is large and predictable. About 70% of Martha's Rule calls do not concern deterioration. In a US "Condition Help" programme, 83% of calls were non-safety issues, most often inadequate pain control ([Eden et al. 2017](https://shmpublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.12788/jhm.2697) [search summary]). That supports Recheck's separate "Question about my wait or care" route to the triage nurse. Patient-input tools also over-call. UK eTriage overtriaged **59.2%** relative to nurse triage ([PubMed 34545027](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34545027/)). A 2026 self-triage app found that higher self-reported pain was strongly associated with overtriage ([J Clin Med 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13566270/) [abstract only]). Awareness is the other failure mode: two years after launch at one North Wales hospital, **93% of 168 patients and relatives had never heard of the service** ([Front Health Serv 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13388555/)). An always-visible button addresses awareness. Its label and a one-line explanation have to make pressing it feel legitimate, not confrontational ([IJQHC 2026 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13458593/)).

One Martha's Rule mechanism is missing from Recheck: **the second review**. In the UK model, a person whose concern has been heard but who is still worried can call a different team. Recheck sends "I'm worried" straight to the triage liaison physician, which suits a waiting room with no treating team. It offers nothing when that first response leaves the person still worried. The brief already says to "agree who provides a second review" (§3). The site has to choose who that is, for example the charge nurse or the backup physician.

The table below sets out what the screen should do, based on all five evidence streams.

| Screen element | What it should do | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| "I need help now" (always visible) | Send immediately with no confirmation step. The urgent screen tells the person to go to the triage desk or nearest staff, or call out. | Low (programme data), but policy-mandated instruction (HCS-181-01 §1.3) |
| "I'm worried I'm getting worse" (always visible) | Route as a clinical concern, never cleared by later answers. After acknowledgement, offer a second review. | Low–moderate (Martha's Rule data, family-concern literature) |
| "Question about my wait or care" | Route to the triage nurse only, with no physician page. | Low (call-mix data) |
| "I'm leaving" | Supplements, never replaces, telling a person. Show after-leaving safety-netting (911/811). | Policy (HCS-181-01 §1.3b) |
| Two wellness questions | Route only on "worse/much worse" and "very poor". "Not sure" is stored as unknown. | Low, with conflicting replication |
| Warning-sign list with explicit "None of these" | Observable signs in plain words. Any urgent sign routes at once, and details come afterwards. | Moderate face validity (public red-flag lists), unvalidated as self-report |
| Pain 0–10, patient only | Track the trend. Never raise urgency on pain alone. | Moderate for NRS validity, low as a trigger |
| Wait information | Show the AHS estimate with its real definition and the time it was updated. Never present it as a personal wait. | Low (surveys, one RCT, one simulation) |
| Circle count | Remove from the routine check-in, or keep it opt-in and outside routing. | Very low (no self-administration evidence) |

## Alberta policy already wrote the patient script

The single most important finding for the copy is that **AHS policy already tells waiting patients what to do**. HCS-181-01 §1.3 requires triage staff to tell every waiting patient to "immediately return to the triage desk" if they feel worse, and to alert a health care professional before leaving ([AHS HCS-181-01](https://publicshare.albertahealthservices.ca/teams/policydocuments/1/clp-ed-assess-reassess-pts-guideline-hcs-181-01.pdf)). The same guideline sets staff reassessment at CTAS 2 every 15–60 minutes within visual range, CTAS 3 every 30–120, CTAS 4 every 60–120, and CTAS 5 every 120. It requires notifying the physician or NP of a significant change. It also says care priority "may be adjusted with acuity change", while the initial CTAS stays on record (§2.2a). The guideline in force is the 12 October 2023 revision. Its scheduled review date of 12 October 2026 has passed, and no newer version was found on 7 November 2026. MyHealth Alberta tells waiting patients to tell the triage nurse if their condition changes and says that the most serious problems are treated first ([MyHealth Alberta](https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/aftercareinformation/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=acv4152)). The Foothills ED page says the sickest people are seen first and that AHS "can't predict how long you will need to wait" ([AHS Find Health, Foothills](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/findhealth/Service.aspx?id=6915&serviceAtFacilityID=1075657)). Recheck's copy should repeat this language, not invent its own. Every post-report screen should end with "go to the triage desk". None should imply that the app replaces it.

Two current strings conflict with this policy. `patient.placeInLine` ("Your answers never change your place in line.") is true of the app's ranking. Under §2.2a, though, staff can change care priority after a change the app surfaced, so "never" overpromises. It can also be read as "there's no point answering". AHS's own framing avoids both problems: the sickest are seen first, and staff decide the order. `desk.body` ("The triage nurse will come to you. You do not need to do anything else.") makes an unconditional promise that cannot be kept under load, and it tells a person who may later feel worse to do nothing. That contradicts §1.3a.

The wait estimate is the most technically wrong string. AHS defines its posted number as an estimate **from triage-nurse assessment to being seen by a physician**. It describes the number as a snapshot recalculated every 2 minutes, notes it does not apply to more seriously ill patients, and says it is not total time in hospital ([AHS ED wait times](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/waittimes/waittimes.aspx)). Recheck's "current estimate for new arrivals" is a Recheck gloss that AHS does not use. It also reads at about grade 12, and "4 h 47 min" invites the patient to subtract their own wait from it. Evidence on showing estimates is mixed. Patients strongly want wait information, and receiving it was linked to higher satisfaction in a Dutch survey ([de Steenwinkel et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8903487/)). A small RCT found that giving a category-average estimate did not change satisfaction ([Almulhim et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11864359/)). A simulation found that **inaccurate predictions increased LWBS** ([JAMIA 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13197183/)). The defensible design states AHS's definition, rounds the figure to words, shows when it was last updated, and says plainly that it is not the person's own wait. Because AHS says the estimate does not apply to more seriously ill patients, whether to show it to CTAS 2 visits is a site decision. Reusing the AHS feed in a pilot needs written permission, because the repo's `ahs.py` docstring records personal, non-commercial terms.

Acute Care Alberta's January 2026 review of the Grey Nuns death supports the staff side of Recheck, not a patient-facing triage claim. Recommendation 15 asks AHS to explore digital tools for clinical decision support "for staff", to identify patients who need more urgent physician assessment. Recommendations 5–6 call for a physician at triage, or a designated physician lead when that role is unavailable ([ACA review](https://www.acutecarealberta.ca/aca-qa-review-2026-01-14.pdf)). The premier said six EDs had triage physicians on 7 November 2026 but named no sites ([Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/12141285/albertas-hospital-triage-program-now-in-operation-danielle-smith/)). Whether Foothills is operating one, and during which hours, is **unconfirmed**. Patient copy should say "a doctor or nurse", never "the triage doctor", and should never tell patients whether they are urgent. No Alberta or Canadian family-activated rapid-response programme was found. AHS Patient Relations (1-855-550-2555, from search snippets) handles complaints after the fact, not real-time escalation. It belongs, if anywhere, as a secondary line on the non-clinical question screen.

Language and privacy obligations constrain the build. Punjabi is Calgary's most common non-official home language ([Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-627-m/11-627-m2022051-eng.htm)), and CBC reporting puts Tagalog second [snippet]. That makes English, Punjabi and Tagalog a defensible first set, with Chinese next. AHS offers interpretation by phone and video, and its language page invites patients to print a language card to request an interpreter ([AHS Languages](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/languages/languages.aspx)). Whether interpretation is free to ED patients was not verified, so copy must not say "free". Under Alberta's Health Information Act, AHS as custodian remains responsible for a vendor acting as information manager. A privacy impact assessment must be submitted to the OIPC before a new system goes live, and that requirement survived the June 2026 amendments ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/pias-shared-custody-ai-highlight-changes-to-albertas-health-information-act)). "We never store your name or health number" is accurate only while visits are synthetic. Once a pilot links Recheck to a Connect Care encounter, "never" becomes misleading.

## The warning-sign list covers stroke and head injury but misses airway, colour and blood

The universal `new_findings` list, "Since you arrived, has any of this started or got worse?", already handles the hardest design problem. The bank stores an explicit "None of these" (the `none-option` extension) and allows "Not sure". That mirrors NHS 111 online, where a user must actively choose "I have none of these" before continuing ([111 online guide](https://primaryhealthcarecentrechopwell.nhs.uk/Resources/getting-medical-help-using-111-online-in-the-nhs-app/) [snippet]). One research note recommended adding "None of these". It is already implemented, so no change is needed. The list's thirteen options map well onto the public red-flag lists for stroke and head injury. Speech, face and one-sided weakness cover the elements of FAST in Recheck's own words. Heart & Stroke licenses FAST only for unmodified, non-commercial campaign use, so not reproducing it is correct ([Heart & Stroke licence](https://www.heartandstroke.ca/stroke/signs-of-stroke/share-the-fast-stroke-signs)). Vision and balance capture what FAST misses: in one Sydney cohort, FAST detected only 58.7% of posterior-circulation strokes, against 97.8% for the vision- and balance-extended version, at the cost of specificity ([Tanglay et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11477353/)).

Four options need plain-language rewording, and each rewrite moves closer to its source. "Worst headache of my life" is a clinical idiom, and it appears in first person on companion screens. The validated construct is an instantly peaking, thunderclap headache ([Ottawa SAH Rule, Perry et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24065011/)). NHS lay wording describes a headache that started suddenly and is extremely painful ([NHS headaches](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/headaches/)). "New problem seeing" should name the types, loss of vision, double vision or blurred vision, as the UBC/NINDS public list and the CEDIS 504 definition do ([UBC SPS3](https://sps3.med.ubc.ca/for-the-public/5-warning-signs-of-a-stroke)). "New or worse chest pain" should add pressure or tightness, the descriptors the Public Health Agency of Canada uses ([Canada.ca heart attack signs](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/diseases/heart-health/heart-attack/signs-symptoms-heart-attacks.html)). "Hard to stay awake" should add "keep eyes open", NHS head-injury emergency wording ([NHS head injury](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/head-injury-and-concussion/)). "Harder to breathe" is fine as a review-level sign but too mild to identify an emergency. NHS emergency wording describes gasping or being unable to get words out ([NHS shortness of breath](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/shortness-of-breath/)). A separate severe option lets routing tell the two apart.

Five observable signals are missing from the universal list, and all appear on public emergency lists. **Skin or lip colour change** (blue, grey, very pale or blotchy) appears on NHS emergency lists for both breathlessness and sepsis. A companion can see it ([NHS sepsis](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sepsis/)). **Swelling of the lips, tongue, face or throat, or trouble swallowing** is the airway sign from Food Allergy Canada materials ([Food Allergy Canada](https://foodallergycanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/Symptoms-Sheet.pdf) [snippet]). **Seizure** is currently asked only in the head-injury and confused groups, but NHS lists it as a call-999 sign after head injury and with headache, and it applies to any complaint. **Coughing up or vomiting blood** corresponds to CEDIS 259 and 655 and to the pulmonary embolism symptoms MyHealth Alberta describes ([MyHealth Alberta PE](https://myhealth.alberta.ca/health/Pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=ue4084) [snippet]). **Sweaty, clammy or very pale** is a heart-attack sign a companion can see (Canada.ca). Lower-specificity candidates are uncontrollable shivering and a sense of doom ("feels like I might die"). Both come from the UK Sepsis Trust and Canada.ca lists, and they belong at review level, not urgent. "Heavy bleeding" has no lay definition. "Bleeding that won't stop" leaves the threshold to staff.

Adding items has a cost. **Every finding currently fires a RED task** in `api/src/recheck/rules.py` (`finding_{code}`, 2-minute acknowledgement). So does a single "Vomiting" or "New problem seeing". Combined with the overtriage evidence above, that means tiering findings (urgent versus review) is a clinical routing decision that must come with any expansion of the list. The current `feeling_now` rule has the opposite gap: "Very poor" fires nothing. Yet that item was the only one with independent predictive signal in the PWQ study.

The complaint-specific follow-ups are mostly sound, but they have two real defects. First, `last_known_well` is triggered only by speech, face or weakness. A vision-only or balance-only (posterior) stroke never gets an onset time. Its bins (<1 h, 1–3 h, 3–6 h, >6 h) also do not align with the Canadian Stroke Best Practices decision points of 4.5, 6 and 24 hours. They have no "woke up with it" option, although CSBP names stroke on awakening and unknown onset explicitly ([CSBP ED evaluation](https://www.strokebestpractices.ca/recommendations/acute-stroke-management/emergency-department-evaluation-and-management)). Second, `vomit_count` counts "since arriving". The Canadian CT Head Rule (≥2 episodes) and NICE NG232 (>1 episode) count since the injury ([Stiell 2001 summary](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2001/1201/p1886a.html); NICE text unverified, HTTP 403). Either the question or the rules must add the count recorded at triage.

Several items need other changes. The chest/back group asks about migrating or radiating pain and abrupt worsening, which are two of the four high-risk pain features in the Canadian aortic syndrome guideline. It omits the other two, tearing or ripping pain and severe or worst-ever pain. The guideline states that its pain-feature weighting rests entirely on expert consensus ([Ohle et al., CMAJ 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7828987/)). The chest/back group also omits a cold, pale, numb limb, which signals a perfusion deficit (CEDIS 011). `pain_new_location` should add "Shoulder" and split "Belly" into upper and lower, because Canada.ca names shoulder spread and upper-belly pain in women. Head injury lacks clear fluid or blood from the nose or ears and behaviour change, both on the NHS emergency list. In the companion version of `more_confused`, "than when staff last checked" assumes knowledge companions don't have. The SQiD informant question compares with the person's usual self. Its sensitivity is modest, 44% in one cancer cohort, so it works as a trigger but not as a rule-out ([BMC Cancer 2021](https://bmccancer.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12885-020-07504-x)).

Companion input is where Recheck adds the most. In PROMISE-TIA, **6.6% of TIA patients were unaware of symptoms that bystanders witnessed**, and those patients were higher risk ([PubMed 32961590](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32961590/)). Caregivers under-report when symptoms are severe, and they agree poorly with patients on psychological states (pain ICC 0.58, depression 0.25) ([J Patient Rep Outcomes 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9107556/)). Two rules follow. A companion's "worse" counts fully and a companion's "fine" clears nothing. Companions should report what they see or hear, not what only the patient feels ([NCATS ObsRO definition](https://toolkit.ncats.nih.gov/glossary/observer-reported-outcomes/)). The bank already restricts pain to the patient and phrases pain follow-ups as "Have they said…". Both are correct.

The circle count is the weakest element on the screen. DelApp, the task it imitates, reached an AUC of 0.89 for delirium in a case-control study of older inpatients. **Trained assessors administered it in every validation.** That study excluded people with severe visual impairment or without spoken English, and specificity against dementia fell to 59–68% ([Tieges et al., PLOS One 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227471)). No study has tested self-administration on a personal phone in an acutely ill population. The people most likely to fail a self-test (delirious, drowsy, low vision, low literacy, non-English) are also the least likely to open it. A wrong or missing count is therefore uninterpretable, and each one currently creates a staff task (`attention_incorrect` and `attention_unanswered`, OVERDUE level). A companion observation item ("more confused or harder to keep awake than usual?") captures the same construct with better support. Staff-administered tools such as the 4AT belong in the staff workflow.

## Screen copy reads at grade 2 but leaks jargon and overuses red

A Flesch-Kincaid pass over 25 patient strings scored about **grade 2.1 overall**. Only `patient.departmentWait` exceeded grade 6, at about 11.9 (measured locally by the design researcher). That comfortably meets the NHS target of a reading age of 9 to 11 ([NHS How we write](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/how-we-write)) and the grade 6–8 targets common in Canada. Formulas cannot catch the real problems, which Canada.ca's 2024 style-guide rewrite acknowledged when it moved from formulas to testing ([Digital Transformation Office](https://digital.canada.ca/2024/10/17/plain-language-updates-to-the-canada.ca-content-style-guide/)). Those problems are service jargon ("clinician", "triage level and complaint group", "estimate for new arrivals", "follow-up") and copy that can be read two ways ("You are checked in" sounds like "you're all set"). "Triage" is a judgement call. AHS uses it in policy and signage, and "triage desk" names a physical place patients can see. Keep it where it names that place, and remove it where it is internal classification.

The urgent card already follows the NHS emergency care-card model. Its heading is one call to action ("Tell a staff member now"), it has no competing links, the dock is hidden, and focus moves to the heading ([NHS care cards](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/design-system/components/care-cards)). Three things undermine it. The **offline state is a safety gap**. "Saved on this phone. It will send when you reconnect." never tells a person in an emergency that staff do not know yet, and the same applies to `urgent.failed` and `outbox.saved`. **Red has too many meanings.** The full-width red "I'm telling staff" button only closes the card, and "Yes, I'm leaving" and ordinary error banners also use red. NHS guidance reserves the emergency treatment for emergencies, aiming to "reassure people, not to scare them". "Answer a follow-up" competes with "go to staff now" unless it is placed visibly after that action.

Three entry points overlap: "Report a change" (home), "I'm worried I'm getting worse" (dock) and "I need help now" (dock). Each needs a distinct purpose a frightened person can tell apart. Renaming "Report a change" to "Tell us about a new symptom" separates it. A related inconsistency is in `desk.notClinical`. It tells anyone who feels worse or is worried to press "I need help now", which sends every worry down the RED path and bypasses the AMBER worry route the rules provide.

The main accessibility defects are in the code, not the words. Single-choice answers are `aria-pressed` toggle buttons with no group, so screen readers get no radio semantics or position. GOV.UK's question-page pattern uses a fieldset whose legend is the heading ([GOV.UK question pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages/)). The dock is fixed with a hard-coded 142 px scroll padding and px font sizes, which puts WCAG 2.2 criteria 1.4.4, 1.4.10 and 2.4.11 at risk at 200% text or with longer Punjabi strings ([W3C 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html)). The Punjabi and Tagalog files are empty. Choosing either leaves the UI in English, the "English only" notice itself appears in English, and `<html lang>` never changes ([W3C 3.1.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/language-of-parts.html)). Tap targets pass, since every target is at least 44 px. Older adults make about **4× the touch errors** of younger adults ([PMC3572909](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3572909/)), so "I'm leaving" should not sit next to "I need help now". Machine translation is not an option for clinical questions. Google Translate rendered ED instructions with potentially clinically significant harm in 2% of Spanish and 8% of Chinese translations ([AHRQ PSNet summary of Khoong et al.](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/assessing-use-google-translate-spanish-and-chinese-translations-emergency-department)). Accuracy data for Punjabi and Tagalog were not found.

## Conclusion

The evidence reverses the usual priority between an app's questionnaire and its buttons. Recheck's always-visible escalation routes, and the instruction to go to the triage desk, rest on Alberta policy and on the strongest UK programme data. The scheduled questionnaire, which looks like the core, is the thinnest layer: one small inpatient study, one failed replication, and no ED waiting-room data at all. The product therefore succeeds or fails on whether a frightened patient or companion finds the right button, understands that pressing it does not replace walking to the desk, and never reads a screen as "you're fine". Clinical wording matters, but it matters less than these three behaviours. A shorter check-in with honest copy is worth more than a longer one with more items. Every new item should be judged by whether a lay person can observe the sign, and by how much alert load it adds, not by clinical completeness.

Recheck's pilot would itself be novel evidence. No published study measures serial phone self-report by adults waiting for an ED physician, completion rates for such prompts, or the alert load of an in-app Martha's Rule button. The metrics the brief already proposes would be first-of-kind data, and a hackathon demo can say so honestly: per-visit response rates, "not sure" and missing answers counted separately from delivery failure, calls per 100 waiting patients, and the share of "worried" reports that changed care.
